Kraken & Coinbase · triangular and cross-exchange · measurement report

The edge is always somewhere you can’t reach it.

Five measurement runs across two exchanges, each reaching further than the last — every tradable symbol on Kraken, then Kraken against Coinbase — plus a sixth that repeated the cross-venue scan at a different hour. The search kept finding larger edge the further it went from liquid markets. Every time, the mechanism that would let you take it turned out to be missing, and the largest number of all was not an opportunity at all.

Look at bars three and four. Hunting progressively deader markets lifted the available edge from 2.51 to 29.99 basis points — until it landed within two hundredths of a basis point of the cheapest fee Kraken charges anyone. The ceiling rose all the way to the floor and stopped there. What that near-coincidence conceals is a harder problem, below.

What the Kraken runs showed

The first run priced every leg against real depth and charged Kraken's real per-pair fees. Over a full hour it never once found a loop worth trading — the best moment of the entire hour still lost 22.99 basis points.

That result alone is ambiguous. A fee-dominated loss could mean either "the market is efficient" or "there is real mispricing, and your fee tier is eating it." Those have opposite implications: the second is fixable with volume, the first is not.

So the second run set fees to zero and measured the raw pricing gap. If mispricing existed, it would appear here unobstructed. It topped out at +2.51 basis points — about 1.3¢ on a $50 loop, before a single fee is paid.

Among liquid pairs, the books are internally consistent to within a fraction of a basis point.

That is what an efficiently arbitraged market looks like. Fee tier was never the binding constraint there — there was simply nothing to collect.

Which leaves one honest objection: liquid pairs are exactly where professionals compete, so of course they are clean. The third run went the other way — dropping the liquidity filter to $1,000 daily volume and tracking 594 symbols across 686 triangles, most of them markets nobody bothers to arbitrage.

That worked, in the sense that it found something. Gross edge rose to +20.72bps, roughly eight times the liquid ceiling, and 12.8% of scans saw the best loop clear +5bps — a threshold the liquid universe essentially never reached.

Against tier‑0 fees of 77.80bps that was still 3.8× short, and 1.4× short even of Kraken's cheapest published tier. So the fourth run went the whole way: every tradable pair on the exchange, including roughly 600 markets turning over less than $1,000 a day — 1,516 triangles across 1,317 symbols.

The edge kept climbing, to +29.99bps. And there it stopped, two hundredths of a basis point from the 29.97bps that three legs cost at the best taker tier. At that tier, the best moment of the run nets +0.02bps — one hundredth of a cent on $50.

It is tempting to read that as nearly there. It is not close at all, and the reason has nothing left to do with fees. It is in what the winning cycle routes through: a market that trades €441 a day, being asked to absorb a €43 leg — about a tenth of its entire daily volume — with two more legs firing behind it.

That fee tier requires around $10M of 30-day volume. The pair offering the edge trades €441 a day.

Capturing every trade in XL1/EUR for a month would still leave you three orders of magnitude short of qualifying for the tier that makes it break even. The edge and the fee tier that would pay for it exist in mutually exclusive parts of the exchange. No amount of capital, speed, or better code reconciles those two facts — they are not a gap to be closed but a contradiction.

Run E — the other exchange

One direction remained. Cross-exchange arbitrage — buy an asset on Kraken, sell it on Coinbase — has wider and more persistent spreads than anything inside a single venue. So the fifth run watched both books at once: 59 assets listed on both, full depth on each side, 1,783 scans.

It is worth being clear that this measures something different. A triangular loop starts and ends in USD on one venue, so a positive number is the market disagreeing with itself. A cross-venue spread is not self-contained: to capture it you must already hold dollars on one exchange and the coin on the other, and every trade drains one side. Periodically you have to move funds back, which costs a withdrawal fee and leaves you exposed while the transfer settles. None of that is in the numbers below.

The scan's top result was +1,104.84bps — nearly 11% — on BONK. It held first place in every heartbeat for fifteen minutes straight.

It is not tradeable, and the reason it is not tradeable is the reason it exists.

Kraken lists BONK as withdrawal_only: the coin can leave Kraken but cannot be deposited into it. So nobody can buy BONK cheaply on Coinbase and bring it to Kraken's higher bid. The gap has no mechanism to close, which is precisely why an 11% gap can sit there in public all afternoon.

The book was real — 10.7 billion BONK resting on Coinbase's best ask, about $26,000 of genuine depth, verified against the raw feed. A scanner that only reads prices would have reported the find of the day. The scanner now checks funding status on both venues before believing any number.

Checked again a day later: the gap is still open, and still shut.

At 16:10 the following afternoon, Kraken still listed BONK as withdrawal_only and the spread was still there — buy Coinbase at 2.43e-06, sell Kraken at 2.537e-06, a +4.40% gap sitting unarbitraged for more than twenty-four hours. That is the prediction this section makes, holding up: a price difference nobody can act on does not decay, because nothing exists to close it.

Its size moved a great deal though, down from roughly 11%. Read the headline figure as a snapshot, not a constant — the mechanism persists, the magnitude wanders.

The second way a cross-venue number lies

A later run turned up something larger than BONK and considerably worse: +43,432 basis points on VELO. Four hundred and thirty-four percent, holding first place scan after scan.

They are not the same coin. Kraken's VELO is Velo Protocol; Coinbase's VELO is Velodrome Finance.

Two unrelated tokens that happen to share a ticker, trading at $0.0033 and $0.0174. The scanner matched them by symbol, compared the prices, and reported the difference as free money.

This is the more dangerous of the two failures. A blocked-transfer gap like BONK simply cannot be captured — you try, and nothing happens. A ticker collision is worse: the trade executes. You buy Velo Protocol on Kraken, then instruct Coinbase to sell Velodrome you have never owned, and what you are left holding is not a hedge but two unrelated positions and a loss.

Nothing already in the scanner would have caught it. The transferability filter answers “can this coin move between the venues”, not “do both venues mean the same coin”. The fix compares a reference price from each exchange at startup and drops any pair diverging by more than 25% — genuine cross-venue spreads live in basis points, so nothing real is anywhere near that band. Dropped pairs are reported loudly rather than skipped in silence.

Building that check had its own trap worth recording. Kraken's ticker feed is keyed by REST pair name rather than altname, and the first attempt let USDT/JPY overwrite USDT/USD — so a yen price was compared against Coinbase dollars and USDT was flagged as a 15,932% collision. A guard against bad data is itself data handling, and gets the same scrutiny.

This is the first row in the whole report that clears — and it is worth being precise about what it would take. Both venues at their very best taker tiers, on the single best observation of 1,783 scans, ignoring every transfer cost. +7.56bps on $50 is 3.8 cents.

A single $1 transfer fee erases the profit from about twenty-six such trades.

And every trade moves you further out of balance — dollars pile up on one venue, coin on the other — so the transfers are not optional. For the typical scan rather than the best one, the edge was nearer 10bps, or 5 cents on $50, and the arithmetic gets worse from there. This is the cost the spread was compensating for all along.

Postscript — the same scan two hours later

Run E was repeated from the packaged executable at 23:41 rather than 21:11, same venues, same $10,000 volume floor, 1,785 scans over 58 assets. It did not reproduce Run E. It found considerably more: the best transferable edge reached +84.94bps on COTI, close to four times Run E's +22.55bps, and the share of scans whose best transferable loop cleared +20bps went from 0.3% to 43.3%.

Two things follow, and only one of them is comfortable. The cross-venue spread is strongly time-varying, so Run E was not a worst case and this is not an upper bound either — a fifteen-minute window is a small sample of a moving quantity. And +84.94bps lands 0.9 basis points under the 85.84bps that two taker legs cost at base tiers: the same near-miss the Kraken runs produced against their own fee floor, arrived at by an entirely different route, on a different pair, on a different exchange.

The BONK artifact reproduced exactly, at +1,229.84bps, still correctly quarantined by the transferability filter. Two assets (LINK, LTC) were dropped when Coinbase refused their subscriptions, and the run reports that rather than quietly measuring 58 while claiming 60.

Three samples change the reading. Runs F and G agree closely across seventeen hours and a different time of day, which makes run E look like the low outlier rather than the baseline — the opposite of what two points suggested. On this evidence the typical peak is nearer 80bps than 22, and more than half of all scans in run G found a loop clearing +20bps, so it is not a rare tail either.

It still does not clear. Two taker legs cost 85.84bps at base tiers, so +79.65 falls 6.2bps short — the third time in this report that the best available edge has landed just under the relevant fee floor, and the closest yet on a genuinely transferable asset. Three fifteen-minute windows remain a small sample of a moving quantity; this is a sharper estimate, not a settled one.

Method, and what would undermine this

Runs A–D walked real depth-10 order books from Kraken's websocket v2 feed; run E added Coinbase's level2 feed alongside it, keeping a 200-level window per product. All were sized at $50 and rejected any book older than 1,500ms — so nothing scored here was a frozen quote on a dead market. Fees were charged against the output of every leg. Nothing was ever ordered: every run was read-only and used no API keys on either venue.

Run E additionally checks funding status on both exchanges — Kraken's Assets endpoint and Coinbase's currencies endpoint — and reports the transferable and unfiltered results separately. Without that check its headline figure would have been 11%.

Caveats worth holding against the conclusion ▾
  • Not a controlled A/B. The runs were consecutive, not simultaneous, and covered deliberately different universes (80 triangles / 60 symbols, 76 / 58, 686 / 594, then 1,516 / 1,317) as the liquidity filter was relaxed.
  • Runs A and B were interrupted. The machine entered Windows Modern Standby mid-run — roughly 9 minutes in run A, and run B collected 416 scans where ~1,500 were expected. Runs C and D were clean, holding 556–576 scans in every five-minute window.
  • The +29.99bps peak is a tail observation. It is the maximum over 1,516 triangles sampled 2,219 times — roughly 3.4 million cycle evaluations — so the extreme carries heavy selection. Nearly half of run D's scans still had a best cycle under +5bps.
  • Market impact is not modeled, and it dominates here. The depth walk consumes quoted levels but assumes they persist across three sequential orders. On a €441/day book absorbing a €43 leg, that assumption is doing more work than any other number in this report.
  • The scanner does not enforce ordermin/costmin. The bot checks them before trading, but the reported edges do not — some observations in runs C and D may name cycles Kraken would reject. The run D winner was checked by hand and passes.
  • $50, depth-10, taker only. Larger size is strictly worse, since deeper fills reach worse levels. Maker rebates are not modeled — that is a different strategy with different risk, not a cheaper version of this one.
  • Fee tiers are Kraken's published schedule, not quotes obtained for this account. The 0.10% best-tier figure is the relevant one for the break-even argument and is treated as a floor.
  • Coinbase's fee schedule is assumed, not verified. Kraken's tier-0 rates came from its own API during run A. The Coinbase columns in the two-leg table are published-schedule figures entered by hand — no account was used. Treat that table as a range to locate yourself in, not a quote.
  • Run E's costs are understated by design. Withdrawal fees, on-chain fees, and the price risk of an in-flight transfer are not modeled at all. For cross-venue trading these are not a correction to the answer — they are the dominant term.
  • Run E lost one asset. Coinbase caps level2 subscriptions per connection; the feed shards across four, and one subscription was still refused, dropping LTC. 59 of 60 were measured, and the scanner now reports any such gap rather than quietly measuring a subset.
  • Asset identity is matched by ticker, then price-checked. Neither exchange exposes a canonical asset ID the other shares, so the scan pairs symbols and rejects any pair whose prices diverge past 25%. That catches gross collisions like VELO; it would not catch two genuinely distinct tokens that happen to trade at similar prices. Treat the asset list as screened, not verified.
  • Cross-venue spreads move with the clock. Runs E and F were the same scan two hours apart and differed by roughly 4× in peak transferable edge. A fifteen-minute window is a small sample of a quantity that varies; neither run bounds the other.
  • Transferability is a point-in-time check. Deposit and withdrawal status can change hourly, and an asset enabled on both venues can still have a slow or expensive network. The filter catches hard blocks, not friction.
  • Two venues, not the market. This says nothing about other exchanges, perpetuals, or DEX routes, each of which carries its own version of the same accounting.

The bot itself works correctly — 160 minutes, 16,376 passes, two exchanges, up to 1,317 live order books at once, pricing honestly throughout. Each run found more edge than the last, and each time the reason it could not be taken turned out to be different: fees that exceed it, markets too small to hold the trade, a deposit channel that is simply closed. The most valuable thing it did was refuse to report an 11% opportunity it could see in the data. Declining to call a big number an opportunity is the behaviour you want from an instrument you would otherwise trust with money.
